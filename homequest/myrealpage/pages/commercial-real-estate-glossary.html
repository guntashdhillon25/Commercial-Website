Commercial real estate glossary

The terms you’ll see on commercial listings and leases, with examples.

Additional rent

On most commercial leases you pay two amounts: base rent for the space, and additional rent for your share of the building’s property taxes, insurance and upkeep (snow removal, landscaping, repairs).

Both are quoted per square foot per year. Additional rent is an estimate and is adjusted each year to match actual costs. You may also see it called TMI or CAM.

Example: 1,450 sf × ($32 base + $11.50 additional) ÷ 12 = about $5,256 a month, plus GST.

Cap rate

The cap rate tells you how much yearly income a property earns compared with its price, before any mortgage. It’s the quickest way to compare investment properties.

Higher cap rates mean more income per dollar but often more risk. Lower cap rates usually mean safer tenants or a better location.

Example: $271,600 income ÷ $4,850,000 price = 5.6% cap rate. For every $100 you pay, the property earns about $5.60 a year.

Clear height

The usable height inside a warehouse, from the floor to the lowest thing hanging from the ceiling (beams, sprinklers). It decides how high you can stack racking.

Confidentiality agreement (NDA)

Owners keep the sale of a business quiet so staff, customers and competitors don’t find out early. Before sharing the name, address and financial records, they ask buyers to sign a short agreement to keep it private.

Signing costs nothing and does not commit you to buy.

Due diligence

The checking period after your offer is accepted and before you are committed. You review things like the building’s condition, environmental reports, leases, financial records and permits.

Offers are usually made “subject to” due diligence, so you can walk away if something important turns up. It typically takes 30 to 60 days.

Financing commercial property

Lenders usually ask for a larger down payment on commercial property than on a home, often 25% to 35% of the price. Rates are typically a little higher, and terms are shorter.

If you’ll run your own business from the property, you may qualify for a lower down payment through programs such as BDC. An advisor can connect you with lenders who do commercial loans.

Gross lease

A lease where one rent covers everything: space, property taxes and building costs. The number you see is the number you pay (plus GST).

Gross leases are easier to budget for, but the landlord builds the cost of taxes and upkeep into the rent.

GST on commercial real estate

Commercial rent and the purchase of commercial property usually have 5% GST added. If your business is GST-registered, you can normally claim it back.

Net lease

A lease where you pay base rent plus your share of the building’s costs (additional rent). It’s the most common type for commercial space in BC.

The advertised rent looks lower than a gross lease, so compare the total monthly cost, not just the base rent.

Net operating income (NOI)

The rent a property collects in a year, minus the costs of running it (taxes, insurance, repairs, management). It does not include mortgage payments.

Offer to lease (LOI)

A short document that sets out the main terms you want: rent, length of lease, start date and any work the landlord will do. Once both sides agree, a lawyer prepares the full lease.

Owner’s cash flow (SDE)

Seller’s discretionary earnings: the money a business makes for one full-time owner before loans and taxes. It adds back the owner’s own pay and one-time costs so you can see what the business really earns.

Small businesses usually sell for about 1.5 to 3 times their SDE.

Example: Asking $185,000 ÷ $142,000 SDE = 1.3×. At this cash flow the price would be paid back in a little over a year, before taxes and loans.

Price per square foot per year

Commercial rents are quoted as dollars per square foot per year, not per month. To get a monthly figure, multiply by the size and divide by 12.

Example: $32/sf/yr on 1,450 sf = $46,400 a year = $3,867 a month in base rent.

Property transfer tax

A BC tax paid by the buyer when the property changes hands. For commercial property it is 1% of the first $200,000 and 2% of the rest. It is due on completion, so plan for it in your cash needs.

Second-generation restaurant space

A space where a restaurant operated before and left the expensive kitchen parts behind: hood, fire suppression, grease trap and plumbing.

It can save months of permits and a large part of the build-out cost compared with starting from an empty shell.

Strata

You own your unit, and share ownership of the common areas (roof, lobby, parking) with other owners. A monthly strata fee pays for their upkeep.

Ask for the strata minutes and depreciation report during due diligence: they show upcoming repairs and fee increases.

Zoning

Zoning is the city’s rulebook for what a property can be used for: a café, a warehouse, an office and so on. If your business isn’t allowed in the zone, you can’t open there without a rezoning, which can take a year or more.

Before you sign a lease or an offer, ask the city (or us) to confirm your specific use is allowed.
